One month later, money doesn't mean what it used to.
A bag of rice that cost two dollars now trades for a pair of boots. Or a phone. Or a week of work.
In parking lots behind closed stores, a new kind of market appears. No prices. No receipts. Just people swapping whatever they have for whatever they need.
In the city, every patch of soil becomes a garden. Balconies. Rooftops. Even the park where you used to go jogging. Tomatoes in buckets. Potatoes in old tires.
You plant some too. But seeds take months to become food... and hunger doesn't wait.
So people start leaving. Long lines of cars head out of the city, toward farms, toward relatives in villages, toward anywhere that grows something.
The farmers do have food. What they don't have is fuel, fertilizer, or spare parts for their tractors.
And for the first time, you realize the problem was never just the trucks. It was everything connected to them.
