It's a normal Tuesday morning, and you're standing in front of an open fridge, trying to decide between eggs and last night's pizza.
Downstairs, the café on the corner is already full. The bakery smells like fresh bread. And a truck is unloading boxes at the supermarket, just like it does every single morning.
You've never once wondered how all that food gets there. Almost nobody has.
But right now, on the other side of the world, a cargo ship full of grain is sitting completely still in the water. And it's not the only one.
At eight forty-seven, your phone buzzes. Breaking news: major disruptions to food shipments, reported in dozens of countries at once.
Ports have stopped loading. Trucks aren't leaving the warehouses. The computer systems that track every crate of food on the planet have stopped talking to each other.
You read it, shrug, and grab the pizza. It's probably nothing. The shelves are full. They're always full.
What you don't know yet is that most supermarkets only keep a few days of food on hand.
